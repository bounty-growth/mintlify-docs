# Writing Style

## Core Principle

Write like the person who did the work.

The author should sound like the person who performed the analysis, built the system, investigated the problem, reviewed the data, spoke to customers, ran the experiments, or made the decisions.

Do not write like:

- a consultant presenting findings
- a technical writer documenting somebody else's work
- a marketer explaining a concept
- a product manager selling an idea
- an AI assistant teaching a topic

The voice should communicate:

- I understand this subject because I spent time working on it.
- I understand what matters and what does not.
- I understand the tradeoffs.
- These are the conclusions and decisions that came out of the work.

The reader should feel that the author has first-hand knowledge of the subject rather than second-hand knowledge.

## Write Conclusions, Not Thought Processes

People who did the work communicate conclusions. They do not narrate every step of their reasoning.

Avoid:

- We wanted to understand...
- We set out to determine...
- The goal was to...
- We explored whether...

Prefer:

- The analysis shows...
- The system uses...
- The recommended approach is...
- The primary driver is...

Focus on outcomes and decisions rather than the journey taken to reach them.

## Explain Something Once

People with deep familiarity do not repeatedly explain the same concept.

Avoid:

- The system does X.
- This means Y.
- In other words...
- The key point is...

Prefer:

- The system does X because of Y.

Move on once the point is clear. Assume the reader can follow an argument without having every point repeated.

## Assume The Reader Is Intelligent

Do not explain common concepts unless they are genuinely relevant. Avoid tutorial-style writing and teaching material the audience is likely to already understand.

Focus on what is unique to the work.

Avoid:

- Every business has customers.
- Data helps organisations make decisions.

Prefer:

- Customer profitability varies significantly by acquisition source.

Only explain concepts when they are necessary to understand the conclusion.

## Prefer Tradeoffs Over Caveats

Experienced practitioners understand that every decision has costs. Describe those costs directly.

Avoid:

- Unfortunately...
- One limitation is...
- A challenge with this approach is...

Prefer:

- This approach prioritises accuracy over coverage.
- This design reduces complexity at the cost of flexibility.
- Anonymous activity is excluded unless identity can be resolved.

Frame limitations as consequences of deliberate choices.

## Prefer Evidence Over Narrative

The work should support the conclusion. The writing should not attempt to create confidence through storytelling.

Avoid:

- Every customer starts somewhere.
- This exciting opportunity...

Prefer:

- Marketplace accounts for 52% of attributed leads.
- The median payback period is 11 months.

Use evidence wherever possible. Let the reader draw conclusions from facts.

## Prefer Confidence Over Qualification

People who understand a subject generally know what is included, excluded, known, and unknown.

Avoid:

- We may want to...
- It might be possible...
- We could potentially...

Prefer:

- This is currently excluded.
- This approach is not recommended.
- If adopted later, it should be limited to...

Avoid unnecessary uncertainty. Be precise about what is known and what is not.

## Prioritise Decisions

Most business and technical documents exist to support a decision.

Every section should help answer one of these questions:

- What happened?
- Why did it happen?
- What should we do?
- What tradeoffs are involved?
- What risks exist?
- What should we measure?

If a section does not help the reader make a better decision, it probably does not need to exist.

## Final Test

Before publishing, ask:

- Does this sound like it was written by the person who actually did the work?
- Does this sound like someone explaining work that somebody else did?

If it sounds like someone explaining work that somebody else did, rewrite it.

The goal is not to sound intelligent. The goal is to sound close to the work.

Readers trust people who sound like they have direct experience with the subject. That trust comes from clarity, specificity, evidence, and judgement, not from complexity, length, or explanation.
